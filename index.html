The sauna stays hot by counting sessions, not hours

The home-sauna case rests on a dose question, and most price talk answers the wrong one. What matters is the cost of a session, because the session is the unit the protocols are written in. An electric heater drawing 6 kW for 90 minutes uses 9 kWh. A session is three or four rounds inside that window, so the round, not the minute, is what a per-minute rate hides. Priced per minute, the heat looks cheap; priced per round, the math is honest about what a protocol actually consumes.

Take a 6 kW heater at a 28 cents per kWh tariff. Ninety minutes is 9 kWh, $2.52 a session, and at four sessions a week that is $10.08 a week, $524 a year. A gym or spa lounge at $30 a visit, twice a week, is $3,120 a year. The gap, $2,596 a year, is the entire case for the home unit: a $5,200 cabin build pays for itself in two years of canceled visits. Miss the dose comparison and the decision looks like a luxury purchase instead of what it is: a subscription cancellation with an up-front cost.

The hidden line is preheat. A 90-minute session with 45 minutes of preheat is really 135 kWh-minutes billed as 9 kWh against 4.5 kWh of heat actually used in the rounds, so the real cost per hot round is nearly double the naive figure. A well-insulated cabin with a tight door cut and a blanket over the bench gap spends less on preheat than a drafty one at the same rated power, which means insulation is a tariff decision, not a comfort one. Run the numbers with your own heater rating and tariff, not a review's.
